# Chapter 9: Being Upset Doesn't Buy You a Free Pass

You are at dinner with friends; the table is full, and the evening has been good, filled with easy conversation and laughter—the kind of relaxed atmosphere that makes you forget the week behind you. And then your partner makes a cutting remark at your expense—small, maybe, a joke with an edge—but it lands wrong, in front of everyone.

The heat rises, the Little Monster fires, and right behind it, the Big Monster's pitch arrives—and it sounds so reasonable.

*You are this upset, which means something real just happened. It would be unfair to hold it in, so you have every right to say something. Let it out right now.*

Notice what just happened in that sentence—it moved fast, so fast you probably didn't catch it. It started with something true—*you have every right to say something*—and ended with something very different—*let it out, now.* Two separate claims, welded together so cleanly that they felt like one.



The first claim is true—you do have a right to say something. Whether a partner makes a cutting remark in front of friends, a colleague takes credit for your work, or a friend cancels on you for the third time, you have a legitimate grievance. The feeling is real, the line was crossed—nothing in this book has ever argued otherwise, and nothing will.

The second claim is different—it's about delivery. It says that because you have the right to speak, you must speak right now, in this way, with the heat still in your voice, and that the upset itself grants a free pass on timing, tone, and method—if you don't let it out while it's hot, you're somehow betraying yourself.



## What Being Upset Actually Grants

Let's be honest about what being upset does give you, because it's more than nothing—it gives you a signal. When the heat rises, something in your environment has crossed a line, and that's real information. The body is telling you: *pay attention, this matters.* It's not a malfunction; it's a detection system.

Being upset also gives you motivation—the energy to address something rather than let it slide, the push to speak up when you might otherwise stay quiet. That's useful, and it's the raw material for intentional assertion—the clear, deliberate voicing of a boundary that this book has always respected.

And being upset gives you legitimacy—the feeling itself is valid. It doesn't need to be suppressed, argued away, or apologized for. You're not wrong for being angry when someone crosses a line; the anger is evidence that something inside you registered a violation.

What being upset does not give you is a specific delivery method—it doesn't dictate your tone, your timing, or that you must speak right now, in this moment, with the heat still in your voice. The heat is information about the crossing, not instructions about the response.

The Big Monster has been telling you the opposite—that the heat itself is a mandate, the intensity of the feeling is proof that the delivery must match it, and if you're this upset, holding it in would be a kind of self-betrayal. But the heat doesn't carry instructions—it carries information. What you do with that information is still yours to decide.

## The Fairness Test

Now let's look at the actual claim the Big Monster makes: *It wouldn't be fair to hold this in when I'm this upset.*

Fair is an interesting word, so let's apply a simple test to it.



The implication is that holding it in—waiting, choosing a different moment, or letting the heat pass before speaking—would be unfair to you, acting as a denial of your experience, a suppression of your voice, and a quiet acceptance of something unacceptable.

But notice what's actually being compared: the Big Monster frames it as a choice between speaking and not speaking—*either you let it out right now, or you hold it in and stay silent forever.* That's the two-door trap from Chapter 6, wearing a different costume. The binary is fake.

The real choice is never between speaking and silence; it is between speaking now while hijacked or speaking in a few minutes when fully present. It's between delivering the message in the voice that comes from emergency mode, or delivering it in the voice you actually choose.

Holding it in for a few minutes doesn't deny the grievance or silence the truth, nor does it require you to pretend nothing happened; it simply delays the delivery by a small amount of time. And what do you get for that delay? You get your full mind back online, access to the nuance and context that the narrowing stripped away, and the ability to choose words that will actually serve what you want—which, as we saw in Chapter 5, is to be understood and respected, not just to discharge heat.

What is actually unfair about choosing when and how to speak, or giving yourself the advantage of a fully engaged mind? The word "fair" is doing dishonest work here—it's implying that the current moment, the heated moment, is the only legitimate one. But the heated moment is the worst possible time to deliver a message you actually want heard.

## The Weld Breaks



Your right to speak is real, your grievance is valid, and your anger is legitimate, yet none of that alters the underlying reality. But being upset never determined the method. The Big Monster fused permission and delivery because it needed the heat to feel like an obligation—it needed you to believe that feeling angry and speaking sharply were the same act.

They're not—and once the weld breaks, you can hold both truths at once.

You have every right to say something. And you get to choose how and when you say it.

The anger doesn't own the delivery, the heat doesn't own the timing, and the upset doesn't own the tone; those choices are still yours. They always were. The Big Monster just didn't want you to know that, because a person who knows they can choose is a person who is harder to hijack.

Being upset gives you the right to speak, but it doesn't dictate the how or the when. Permission and delivery are two different things.

But the voice has one more trick: it says if you don't speak right now, you'll lose your nerve and never say it at all. That's the next illusion — and it's the one that keeps the cycle running.
