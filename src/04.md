# Chapter 4: The Relief That Isn't Yours to Take Credit For

The words are out, the sentence has landed, and for a split second, the room is silent.

Then comes the flood of release, as your shoulders drop and the breath that had been shallow and tight in your chest finally opens up. The pressure that had been building behind your sternum — the one you'd been carrying since the moment the trigger fired — is suddenly gone. There's a strange, almost physical satisfaction—*finally, finally I said it, finally I got it off my chest.*

It feels good, and there's no point pretending it doesn't. In fact, this book is not going to pretend anything about that relief—it's real, it happens in your body, and it can be measured in loosened muscles, slowed breathing, and the sudden, quiet clarity that rushes in after the storm.

But here's the uncomfortable thing: that exact same feeling of relief follows an outburst that achieves nothing. It follows an outburst that makes everything worse—one you regret within minutes, that damages a relationship, entrenches a reputation, and leaves you lying awake replaying the words, wishing you could take them back. The feeling arrives anyway—just as strongly after the worst, most destructive snap as after the one you think was justified.



It tells you that the relief isn't evidence that the outburst was necessary or that anything was resolved—the relief is doing something else entirely. And once you see what that something is, the Big Monster's favorite promise—*you'll feel so much better once you get this off your chest*—starts to look less like a truth and more like a magic trick.



Imagine you've been wearing a shoe that pinches all day—not a catastrophic injury, just a constant, low-grade pressure against the side of your foot. By the end of the day, you're aware of it with every step, and it's irritating, distracting, slowly building a tension in your whole body as you compensate for the discomfort.

Then you get home, sit down, and pull the shoe off.



But here's the question: is that relief evidence that the shoe was doing something useful—that the pinching was necessary, the discomfort was serving a purpose, and you should put the shoe back on and keep wearing it tomorrow?

Of course not; the relief is simply evidence that something uncomfortable has stopped. The pain and the relief were generated by the same source: the shoe created the pain, removing the shoe created the relief, and neither one of those things proves the shoe was good for you.

Now let's apply this to the moment you snap.

The trigger fires—a partner makes a cutting remark at the dinner table, a colleague interrupts you for the second time in ten minutes, a friend cancels plans for the third time in a row by text, an hour beforehand.

And then the Big Monster arrives with its interpretation. *This is an emergency and an injustice. You are being disrespected, and if you don't respond right now, you will be telling them it's acceptable, so you cannot let this pass and must say something immediately.*

That interpretation creates a massive internal tension—not just the Little Monster's physical heat, that racing pulse and hot neck that would subside on its own in a few minutes, but a cognitive tension, a story-driven pressure. The Big Monster has declared an emergency, and emergencies demand action—the longer you don't act, the more the tension builds. *Say it right now, and you'll feel better once it's out.*

Then you snap—the words fire, the action happens.

And the tension that the interpretation created is suddenly discharged—the emergency, which existed only in the story, is over because you acted. The relief you feel is real, but it is the relief of removing a pinching shoe—the pain and the release came from the same source: the Big Monster's story.

The outburst didn't resolve anything with the other person—it neither made you feel more understood, respected, or safe, nor did it fix the interrupted meeting, the cutting remark, or the cancelled plans. In most cases, it made all of those things worse. But it did end the internal emergency and shut down the story's demand—and that's what the relief actually is: not the satisfaction of a problem solved, but the cessation of a self-generated alarm.

The Magic Trick is this: the Big Monster creates the tension, and then takes credit for the relief when the outburst ends it. It's like a magician who sets the stage on fire and then expects applause for putting it out—the fire was never necessary and putting it out was never an achievement, making the entire spectacle an illusion from start to finish.

And this is why the relief has never lasted—because the shoe is still there the next morning. The actual situation—the colleague, the partner, the friend, the grievance—hasn't been addressed; it's still waiting, unresolved. The relief you felt was real, but it was relief from a tension that existed only in your head—the underlying problem remains untouched.

Now let's look briefly at what happens when you try to get the relief without snapping.

You know the drill: the trigger fires, the Big Monster starts its pitch, but instead of snapping, you go to a friend and vent. Perhaps you write out an angry text only to delete it, or spend an hour replaying the argument in your head, perfecting the comeback and imagining how good it would feel to say it.

And it does feel like release, for a little while—the pressure drops, the tension eases. You think you've processed it and let it out.

But the mechanism is identical: the venting didn't resolve the issue—it discharged the tension created by the Big Monster's interpretation. And worse—it rehearsed the circuit. Every time you vent or mentally loop the argument, you strengthen the very story that created the tension in the first place, practicing the belief that the trigger was an emergency, a response was required, and your dignity was at stake.

Rather than processing the emotion, you are merely practicing it. The venting feels like release, but it is really another round of training where each rehearsal makes the Big Monster's story feel more true, familiar, and automatic. The circuit gets deeper, and the next trigger finds it more easily.

The relief after snapping is real—it just isn't evidence of anything except that a self-generated emergency has ended. And once you see that, the promise—*you'll feel so much better once you get this off your chest*—loses its power, because you know what you're actually feeling better from. You're not releasing the issue; you're releasing a tension that the interpretation manufactured. If the interpretation hadn't been believed in the first place, there would have been nothing to release.

If the relief isn't real resolution, what was the actual need the outburst was reaching for? That's the next question — and the answer changes what "being heard" actually requires.
